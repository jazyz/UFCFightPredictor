Kalshi UFC Playbook

How much edge the model needs to clear Kalshi's fees, and four ways to turn that edge into money on a limit-order book: taking, posting, quoting both sides, and managing the fills that go wrong. Every cost is stated in the market's own unit, cents per $1 contract, which is the same thing as probability points.

Prepared 2026-09-02 · replay of 175 tier-2 walk-forward bets, 2024-01 → 2026-07 · 126 Kalshi quotes in the odds file since 2026-05

Fees are not the binding constraint. Overconfidence on underdogs is.

Buying a contract at the market's fair price and holding to settlement costs about 1.75¢ at even money in taker fee, and less at the extremes. Lifting the offer in a one-cent-wide book adds one more cent. So a taker needs roughly 2 to 3 probability points of real edge to break even, depending on price. A resting bid that gets filled one cent inside the market pays no fee and starts one point ahead.

The model's picks beat the de-vigged sportsbook price by 7.7 points on hit rate across the 175 backtest bets. That clears every route on average. It does not clear the taker route where the model is most confident: on picks priced under 40¢ the realized edge is 1.2 points against a 2.5 to 3 point cost. The instrument that fixes this is a shrink on the model's stated edge, not a change of venue.

Fee math

Kalshi charges a fee when an order trades, not when a contract settles. The fee on a trade is a fixed coefficient times price times one-minus-price, per contract, rounded up to the next cent on the order total. Because p·(1−p) peaks at 50¢, the fee is largest at coin-flip prices and shrinks toward both ends. The taker pays it. On most markets the maker pays nothing.

The schedule that applies to UFC, verified against the exchange API on 2026-09-02

Kalshi's fee schedule, effective 7 July 2026, defines the taker fee as round up(M × 0.07 × C × P × (1 − P)), where C is the contract count, P the price in dollars, and M a per-series multiplier that defaults to 1. A maker fee of 0.0175 × C × P × (1 − P) exists but applies only to series flagged for it. The series metadata for the UFC fight-winner contract, ticker KXUFCFIGHT, reports fee type quadratic with multiplier 1. That is the taker-only table. The method-of-victory and round contracts carry the same type. No fee change is scheduled for any series, and none of the 31,000 active liquidity-incentive programs on the exchange covers a UFC or MMA market.

  • Posting on UFC markets is free. No maker fee, and also no maker rebate. The NFL, NBA, NHL, tennis and golf series do charge makers a quarter of the taker rate; fight markets do not.
  • The fee is charged per fill, on the way in and on the way out. There is no settlement fee on a yes/no contract. A position held to the bell pays once; a position flattened before it pays twice.
  • Rounding is effectively exact for an API account. The engine rounds each fill's fee up to a millionth of a dollar and rebates the round-up across the order; direct members' balances are kept to a hundredth of a cent. The old "one cent minimum per contract" folklore no longer describes what you pay.
  • The exchange keeps trading through the fight. The market closes early only once a result is declared, and settles about five minutes later.

What is public about depth and speed

Volume is concentrated at the top of the card to a degree the one-cent hold hides. Topuria against Gaethje at UFC Freedom 250 traded more than $6.1M on Kalshi, the year's most liquid fight; on the same card Nickal's market held about $69K. Kalshi's own Fight Night listings show undercard markets with volumes of $0, $12 and $666. Makhachev against Garry showed a one-cent spread with 1.7 million contracts traded two days out. The picture is a book that is deep and tight for a main event and essentially empty for the prelims where a model most often disagrees with the market.

On speed, two facts matter for anyone resting orders. Kalshi has no in-play delay on order execution; it filed a proposal for one in December 2025 and described the filing as only a filing. Since 12 August 2026 Kalshi streams its full level-two book to institutional subscribers through a low-latency network, so the makers you compete with see depth changes faster than an API poll can. The one cross-venue study of UFC contracts, 281 fights matched across Kalshi and Polymarket, finds neither venue leads the other; nothing is yet published on whether either lags the sportsbooks, which is the lag that would pay a maker.

Who is on the other side

Becker's analysis of 72 million Kalshi trades through November 2025 puts the transfer at 1.12% per trade from takers to makers, and the sports slice, 43.6 million trades, at the same figure. The sign flipped in the second quarter of 2024, when institutional makers arrived; before that, takers were ahead. The makers are Susquehanna, described as the exchange's flagship market maker and active in sports, Jump, Wintermute and Galaxy, Kalshi's own affiliate at under 6% of sports making volume, and now the sportsbooks themselves: Flutter booked $6M of market-making revenue in the second quarter of 2026 and DraftKings reports making markets on singles and combos at a profit. The fight-winner series sits in Kalshi's designated market-maker program with a 98% hourly availability requirement. A one-cent book on every fight three days out is what a program like that produces.

These firms see the sportsbook consensus in real time, and two of them are the sportsbook consensus. A resting order from this desk is competing with them for the same uninformed flow and standing in front of them when the line moves.

Does Kalshi lag the books?

Nobody has measured it for UFC. The spot checks that exist put liquid Kalshi fight markets within zero to three points of the de-vigged DraftKings price before the fight, with the exchange occasionally a couple of points above the book on a heavy favourite. The one formal price-discovery study with Pinnacle in the panel is on 2024 election contracts and finds the order-book exchanges leading and the dealer books following, which is the wrong direction for a maker hoping to exploit a lag. The one in-play study of Kalshi, on NBA games, finds prices move only 0.64 for each point of public information and drift for fifteen minutes afterwards, but the executable return after spread is negative. Across 387 sports contracts, OddsShopper found no favourite-longshot bias in Kalshi's sports prices at all. The softness on this exchange is in thin books and in-play under-reaction, not in a pre-fight lag to the sportsbooks.

1

Market orders: taking

You cross the spread the moment the model says the price is wrong. You pay for certainty of execution and choose every timestamp yourself. This is the backtest, moved to a cheaper-vig venue that then charges the vig back as a fee.

You earn
The model's edge, net of costs. Realized: 7.7 pts hit-rate edge; +7.9% per bet on the 175 bets when replayed at fair + 1¢ + fee.
You pay
Half the spread plus the fee. 2.25¢ at 50¢, 1.6¢ at 80¢, 1.6¢ at 20¢. Average on our picks: 1.67¢ fee plus the half-spread.
Variance
Per-bet return sd ≈ 106% of stake. Standard error on ROI is 8 pts at n = 175. 700 bets to confirm a 10% edge.
Ends it
Betting the model's raw probability on long dogs. Under 40¢ the realized edge is below the cost of taking.

Mechanics

Compute the fair probability for the side the model prefers. Look at the best offer on that side. If fair minus offer exceeds the required edge, send a limit order at the offer price for the Kelly count with time-in-force immediate-or-cancel, so a book that moved between your read and your order fills you partially or not at all, never worse than the price you chose. Kalshi's current order endpoint has no market order type; every order is a limit, and the taker's tool is an aggressive limit with IOC. That is the right design for a thin book, where a true market order would walk through the 63¢ and 65¢ offers behind the one you saw.

Self-trade prevention is mandatory on every order, so a resting bid of yours on the same market cannot be crossed by your own take. Attach a client order ID; a resend with the same ID returns the original order instead of a duplicate. Hold to settlement. Never sell before the fight unless route 4 says so.

The fair probability is not the blended number the backtest used. Regress outcomes on the model-minus-market gap and use the fitted slope as a shrink. On the current bets the slope is about 0.6, which turns a claimed 13-point edge into 7.7. This is the oldest result in quantitative betting: Benter's 1994 Hong Kong paper reports that in every probability range where his model exceeded the public, actual frequencies fell between the two and closer to the public, and his fix was to fit the model and the market together in a second-stage logit rather than trust either. The 5-point gate the backtest used already exceeds every taker cost stack on the chart, so on average the gate does its job; the problem is that the stated edge and the real edge diverge most exactly where the stated edge is largest.

Variance, in this route's terms

Taking has no fill risk and no adverse selection: you cannot be picked off because you are the one picking. All of the variance is outcome variance, and it is large. Seventy percent of bets are near even money or dogs, so each is close to a coin flip with a payoff near one to one. A 175-bet season has a standard error of eight points on ROI. Nothing you do on the execution side changes that number; only more bets do, or a different measure than ROI.

The measure that converges is closing-line value: the Kalshi mid a minute before the fight, minus the price you paid. Buchdahl's rule of thumb is that per-bet P&L at even money has a standard deviation near 1.0 while per-bet CLV has one near 0.1, so a given edge needs a hundredth as many bets to show up in CLV. A 2-point average CLV is significant at about 96 bets; a 1-point CLV at about 385. Taking with CLV logging is the cheapest experiment in this document, and it is the control arm for everything below.

Risks specific to taking

  • Paying the fee twice. Any exit before settlement is a second trade with its own fee and spread, roughly 2.75¢ round trip at mid prices. Route 4 covers when that is worth it.
  • Buying into a stale book. If nobody has refreshed the offer since a sharp-book move, the offer you lift is the one that just became cheap for a reason. Compare the Kalshi mid to the live sportsbook consensus before every take; treat a gap of more than 3¢ as information, not opportunity.
  • In-play prices. Kalshi keeps trading into and through the fight. An order placed late without an expiry can fill during round one at a price that has nothing to do with the pre-fight model.
2

Limit orders: posting on one side

Same directional view as route 1, executed by resting a bid on the side the model likes and letting someone else cross to you. You give up control of timing and fill to save about three cents a contract. On this model's edge, that is roughly a 40% raise.

You earn
The model's edge plus half the spread, no fee. Upper bound on the 175 bets: +16.7% per bet if every order filled 1¢ inside.
You pay
Nothing in cash. You pay in fills you did not get and in fills you got because news went against you.
Variance
Outcome variance unchanged; fill variance added. Fewer bets per season, each cheaper. Adverse selection shows up as CLV, not as fee.
Ends it
A resting order left unattended through a weigh-in miss or a late replacement.

Mechanics

When the model's shrunk fair price exceeds the current best offer by the required edge, do not lift it. Post a bid at the best bid, or one cent inside it if the queue at the best bid is deep, for the Kelly count. Set the post-only flag, which Kalshi's order endpoint supports: if the book moves before the order lands and the bid would cross, the order is cancelled rather than filled as a take. Send it good-till-cancelled with an expiration time set before the walkout, since an immediate-or-cancel order cannot carry an expiry. Then wait.

Three things can happen. The bid fills because uninformed flow sold into it: you now own the position at a price one to two cents better than route 1 and paid no fee. The bid never fills and the fight starts: you either lift late at the offer, converting to route 1, or you skip. The bid fills because the market moved through it on news: you own a position at what is now a bad price, and route 4 applies.

The right policy for the unfilled case is an empirical question with a clean test. Run the season with half of the unfilled orders converted to takes at the cutoff and half skipped, and compare CLV. My prior is that skipping loses money: the orders that never fill are the ones where the market kept moving toward the model, which are the picks that were most right.

Adverse selection, stated plainly

A resting order is an option you have written for free to everyone else. They exercise it when the price is about to move against you. In fight markets the exercise events are visible: a fighter misses weight, an injury leaks, a replacement is announced, a sharp book moves five cents. An arbitrage bot with that feed hits your stale bid within seconds. Your model updates twice a week.

The defence is not a better model. It is three operational rules. Cancel every resting order when the sportsbook consensus moves more than a set number of cents against it, and reassess before reposting. Never leave orders resting when you are not able to cancel them, which in practice means a process with a heartbeat and cancel-on-disconnect. Size each order so that one full adverse fill is a normal bet, not a season.

Variance, in this route's terms

Fill uncertainty makes the season's bet count random. If a third of orders never fill, the season has 45 bets rather than 69 and the ROI standard error rises from 13 to 16 points. The dollars saved per bet are certain; the number of bets is not. That trade is worth making when the fee saved per fill exceeds the edge forgone on the unfilled orders, which is exactly what the skip-versus-take test measures.

Adverse selection does not show up as fee. It shows up as filled orders with negative CLV. Track CLV separately for orders that filled within an hour of posting and orders that filled later; if the late fills carry the negative CLV, tighten the expiry rather than the price.

3

Two-sided quoting

Post a bid and an offer around a fair value and earn the spread from people who want to trade now. This is a different business from the other three: the money comes from flow, not from being right about the fight, and the model is at best a small skew on a fair value that has to come from somewhere faster.

You earn
Half the spread per fill. On a one-cent book that is 0.5¢ per contract, before adverse selection.
You pay
Every fill that arrives because the price is about to move. One 5¢ move against a stale quote costs ten spread captures.
Variance
Two components: small steady spread income and lumpy adverse-selection losses, plus binary outcome variance on whatever inventory reaches the fight.
Ends it
Quoting off the model instead of off the live sharp consensus. The market is a better fair value than the model at any single instant.

Mechanics

Choose a fair value. Post a bid at fair minus a half-spread and an offer at fair plus a half-spread, both post-only. When one side fills, you hold inventory; lean both quotes away from it so the next fill is more likely to flatten you. Refresh the quotes whenever fair value moves. Repeat until the fight starts, then either be flat or accept that whatever you hold is a bet.

The half-spread has to cover three things: the fee, if the market charges makers one; the expected adverse selection, which is the probability that the next trader knows something times how much the price moves when they do; and a return on the inventory risk you carry. On Kalshi's UFC books the whole spread is one cent. There is no room in that cent for a maker whose fair value is slower than the arbitrage bots' fair value, which is the sportsbook consensus.

Why the model is the wrong fair value here

Across all scored fights the de-vigged market predicts winners better than the model does. The model's edge exists only on the subset of fights where it disagrees with the market by enough, and it materializes only at settlement. A market maker needs a fair value that is right about the next trade, not about the fight. That is the sportsbook consensus, refreshed every few seconds. The model's proper role in this route is a skew: shift both quotes toward the model's side by a fraction of its shrunk edge, so inventory accumulates in the direction you would have bet anyway, and cap how much.

Inventory in a binary market

In a stock you can hold inventory for weeks and hedge it. Here the contract resolves to 0 or 1 at a known time, and there is nothing to hedge it with except the other side of the same book. A maker who is long 200 contracts of the favourite at the walkout has not made a market; they have made a bet with all the variance of route 1 and none of its edge selection. The honest version of this route on a thin book is: quote small, cap inventory per fight at what you would have bet outright, and treat everything you carry into the fight as a route-1 position that must pass the route-1 edge test.

Variance, in this route's terms

Spread income is many small positive numbers. Adverse selection is a few large negative ones. The sum has a fat left tail, and the ratio of the two decides whether the strategy is positive at all. With a half-spread of 0.5¢, breaking even requires that fewer than one fill in ten is followed by a five-cent adverse move. Nobody knows that number for Kalshi UFC books yet; it is the first thing the book logger in the plan below is for.

What the evidence says about makers

The published record is consistent across venues: makers beat takers by a wide margin, and makers still barely make money. Bürgi, Deng and Whelan's study of 46,000 Kalshi contracts through April 2025 finds an average return of −9.6% for makers against −31.5% for takers; the only maker segment in the black is contracts priced at 50¢ or more, at +2.6% with a standard deviation of 33%. Whelan's Betfair study, with a full one-second order book for 152,000 soccer matches, finds pre-match makers at +0.6% and takers at −2.5%; the maker's return has a standard deviation of 198%, and he notes that mean-variance Kelly would stake half a percent of wealth on it. In play, makers who quoted longshots late lost about as badly as the takers who hit them, which is the cleanest published demonstration that late information overwhelms spread capture for anyone quoting passively.

Bartlett and O'Hara's adverse-selection study of 41.6 million Kalshi trades finds that makers in single-name markets earn about 1.9¢ per contract, roughly twice the broad-market figure, and that this profit is cross-subsidized by retail overbuying YES: 61% of single-name volume bought YES and 33% of those markets settled YES. One-sided order flow predicts maker losses in single-name markets. A fight-winner contract is a single-name market.

The Glosten-Milgrom model makes the same point in one formula. For a binary contract with prior p and a fraction μ of perfectly informed traders, the dealer's zero-profit spread is approximately 4·μ·p·(1−p). Kalshi's taker fee of 0.07·p·(1−p) per side is exactly the half-spread a dealer would need if 3.5% of flow were perfectly informed. A one-cent book at 50¢ is a dealer pricing informed flow at about 1%. If more than one trade in a hundred against you comes from someone who has seen the sharp line move, the cent does not cover it.

For sizing a two-sided book, Dalen's 2026 adaptation of Avellaneda-Stoikov to prediction markets works in logit space: the half-spread in probability terms is p·(1−p) times the logit half-spread, and the inventory cap scales with 1 / (p·(1−p)). In words, quote widest and carry least at coin-flip prices, where the payoff is most sensitive to news, and add a jump premium before scheduled information such as the weigh-in.

Verdict

Not with this model as the engine, and not without a live consensus feed. Depth is not the obstacle: main cards carry tens of thousands of contracts within a few cents of the touch three days out. The obstacle is that the other side of a one-cent book on every fight is Susquehanna, Jump, Flutter and DraftKings, quoting off the consensus this desk does not have, under a program that requires them to be there 98% of every hour. If the logger shows that Kalshi mids nonetheless lag the sharp books by minutes, route 3 becomes a latency business in which the model contributes a skew. That is a real business, but it is not the one this model was built for, and the pay per fill is half a cent.

4

Bad fills and inventory

Every route above ends with a position that sometimes goes wrong before the fight. The question is never whether the price moved against you. It is whether the world changed. Exit on information, hold on noise, and know which one you are looking at before the order is live.

You earn
Nothing directly. You keep what the other routes made by not converting paper drawdowns into realized ones.
You pay
Round trip to flatten: cross 1¢ plus fee, about 2.75¢ at mid prices. Hedging on the other side costs the same again.
Variance
Flattening trades outcome variance for a certain small loss. It lowers the season's variance and, if done on noise, its mean.
Ends it
Flattening on price moves. The model's whole thesis is that some price moves are noise; selling into them refunds the edge.

What a bad fill is

A fill is bad when your fair value at the moment of the fill was wrong, and it is worst when it was wrong because someone else already knew. There are three kinds, and they call for different responses.

  1. News. A weigh-in miss, an injury report, a replacement opponent, a cancelled bout. The fight you modelled no longer exists. The model's probability is void. Fair value is now whatever the sharp books say, and if the position is negative against that, flatten. The cost of flattening is a known 2 to 3 cents; the cost of holding is the whole move. The reference case is Minner against Nuerdanbieke in November 2022, where the favourite went from −220 to −420 in the four hours before the fight on a camp leak, about 13 points of implied probability, before books pulled the market.
  2. Sharp money. The sportsbook consensus moved several cents with no public news. Someone with better information than either you or the model is trading. The literature on closing lines is unambiguous that late moves are informative. Treat it as partial news: mark the position to the new consensus, re-shrink the model's edge against it, and hold only if the shrunk edge still clears the gate.
  3. Noise. The Kalshi mid drifted a cent or two on retail flow while the sportsbooks did not move. Nothing happened. Hold. If anything, this is the moment route 2 wants to post more.

Telling the second from the third requires a live sportsbook feed. Without one you cannot distinguish sharp money from noise, and the rule collapses to "hold everything," which is fine for route 1 sizing and fatal for route 3 sizing.

Settlement is not what the backtest assumed

Every ROI figure in this memo comes from a replay that skipped draws and no contests, because a sportsbook voids them. Kalshi's UFC contracts, as reproduced in the rules text available to us, settle a draw or no contest at 50¢ for both sides. A holder of the favourite at 76¢ loses 26¢ on an eye poke. A bout postponed by up to two weeks stays open; a bout cancelled or pushed further is settled at an exchange-determined fair price based on recent trading, not refunded. No clause covering a replacement opponent was found; since the market names both fighters, a substitution most likely falls under cancellation, and that is an inference rather than rule text. In our own fight data, 2.1% of UFC bouts since 2022 ended in a no contest or draw, and 2.7% over the full history. On a portfolio of favourites bought near 65¢ that is a tax of about 0.3 points per bet the backtest never charged, and it is a tax that falls only on the favourite side.

The rules text on a live market, ticker KXUFCFIGHT-26SEP08PASBER-PAS, read from the exchange API on 2026-09-02, says it in the exchange's words: "If this fight is postponed or delayed, the market will remain open and close after the rescheduled fight has finished (within two weeks). If the fight is declared a tie or no contest, the market will resolve to 50/50 for both fighters. If the fight is cancelled or rescheduled to over two weeks away, the market will resolve to a fair price in accordance with the rules." The contract terms define that fair price as the last traded price before cancellation, or an Outcome Review Committee determination if the exchange judges that price unrepresentative. The committee's determinations are final. The method-of-victory contract does address substitutes explicitly, settling at "the last fair price determined at the sole discretion of the Exchange," and the fight-winner contract almost certainly inherits that treatment, but it is not written down.

Two more settlement facts change how inventory behaves. The market keeps trading through the fight and closes early only when a result is declared, so a position can be exited or added to mid-round at prices that reflect the broadcast, and someone with a faster feed than yours is doing exactly that. And a result reversed after settlement, such as a later overturn for a failed drug test, does not reopen the market.

Inventory rules that survive contact

  • Cap per fight before you post. The cap is the route-1 Kelly stake at the shrunk edge. No route may exceed it, including route 3's accidental accumulation.
  • Cap per card. Fights on the same night resolve within hours and the clustered bootstrap shows the season's variance is card-level, not fight-level. A bad card with ten positions is one bad bet wearing ten tickets. Hold total exposure per card to roughly three fights' worth.
  • Mark to the consensus, not to Kalshi. Your unrealized P&L is measured against the sharp books' de-vigged probability, which is the best available estimate of the truth. Kalshi's mid is what you can transact at, not what the position is worth.
  • Build your own dead-man's switch. Kalshi has no cancel-on-disconnect in its REST, WebSocket or FIX interfaces. Two tools substitute for it. Order groups cap the contracts a set of orders may trade in any rolling 15-second window and cancel the whole group when the cap is hit. A cancel-all endpoint pulls every resting order in one call. Run a watchdog separate from the trading process that calls cancel-all if the trader's heartbeat stops. A quote that outlives its author is the one that gets filled at the worst moment.
  • Log every decision with the consensus at that moment. Six months in you will want to know whether your holds beat your flattens. That is only knowable if both were recorded against the same benchmark.

Variance, in this route's terms

Flattening converts a random outcome into a certain small loss, so it always reduces variance. The question is what it does to the mean. Flatten only on news and the mean rises, because you avoid holding positions whose edge is gone. Flatten on any price move and the mean falls by roughly the model's whole edge, because you are systematically selling exactly when the market disagrees with the model, which is the only time the model was ever paid. Kelly sizing already prices the variance you are supposed to carry. Do not pay a second time to remove it.

Before the first order

Everything above rests on three numbers we do not have: the depth at the top of the Kalshi book, the lag between a sharp-book move and the Kalshi mid, and the closing-line value of the model's picks. All three come from the same logger, and none requires capital.

  1. Log the Kalshi book. Best bid, best offer, and size at each, for every UFC market, every few minutes from listing to walkout. The scraper already fetches Kalshi prices from fightodds.io; the exchange's own API gives depth.
  2. Log the sharp consensus alongside it. Pinnacle and Circa if reachable, otherwise the multi-book fightodds.io response that the scraper currently discards. Timestamp both feeds identically.
  3. Compute the shrink. Logistic regression of outcome on model-minus-market gap, walk-forward, refit at each retrain. Use the slope, not 0.8, as the blend.
  4. Run route 1 with CLV logging for a season. Small size, every fight the shrunk edge clears the gate. This is the control.
  5. Run route 2 against it on alternating fights once the logger shows how often a bid one cent inside gets filled and what its CLV looks like.
  6. Revisit route 3 only if the lag measurement shows Kalshi mids trailing the sharp consensus by minutes rather than seconds. Depth on main cards is already sufficient; the lag is the unknown, and no public study has measured it for UFC.

What the exchange gives you to work with

The trading API is sufficient for routes 1 and 2 on the free tier and for route 3 only with an upgrade. Facts verified on the exchange documentation on 2026-09-02:

  • Orders. One endpoint creates orders. Side is bid or ask on YES; selling YES is the same as buying NO. Time-in-force is good-till-cancelled, immediate-or-cancel or fill-or-kill. Post-only is a flag. An expiration time attaches to good-till-cancelled orders. Contract counts go to two decimals, so a $10 stake at 61¢ is a legal 16.39 contracts. Self-trade prevention is a required field.
  • Cancelling. Batch cancel, single cancel, and a cancel-all that pulls every resting order in one request. Order groups provide a rolling 15-second contract cap that cancels the group when exceeded. There is no cancel-on-disconnect; the watchdog is yours to build.
  • Rate limits. Token buckets with separate read and write budgets. The entry tier allows about 10 order writes a second and 20 reads; the next tier is self-serve and triples both. Higher tiers are earned by a share of exchange volume that a UFC-only trader will not reach. Route 3 across a full card at fight-week refresh rates fits inside the second tier.
  • Market data. A WebSocket feed delivers an order-book snapshot followed by sequenced deltas per market, and a private fills channel reports every fill with the fee actually charged. A historical-markets endpoint lists settled UFC markets back to the first one on 8 May 2025. Depth history is not provided and cannot be backfilled, which is why the logger in step 1 has to run forward.
  • Authentication. RSA key pair generated in account settings; each request is signed over timestamp, method and path with RSA-PSS.
  • Position accountability. $25,000 per fighter-market per member, a soft level at which the exchange may ask you to stop adding or to reduce, not a hard reject. Designated market makers get ten times that.
  • Market-maker program. The fight-winner series is a covered product in Kalshi's designated market-maker program, with a 98% availability requirement per hour and confidential incentive terms. No open-to-all liquidity or volume incentive covers any UFC market today, so a small maker on fight contracts earns spread and nothing else.

Eligibility check before anything else. Kalshi accepts US residents, with sports contracts geofenced in a changing list of states that as of this week includes Nevada, Washington, Michigan, Maryland, Ohio, Arizona, Montana, Minnesota, Massachusetts and, by the exchange's own disclaimer, New Jersey and Illinois. Canada is excluded from Kalshi entirely. The Wealthsimple Predict partnership announced in June 2026 brings Kalshi contracts to Canadian residents only in climate, financial and economic categories with settlement at least 30 days out. It does not include sports. If the desk sits in Canada, nothing in this memo is tradable on Kalshi and the same analysis would have to be redone for a venue that is.
